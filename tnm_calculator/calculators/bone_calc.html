Key Imaging Tips

MRI for Local Staging

MRI is the gold standard for local staging of bone sarcomas

Use T1, T2, and fat-suppressed sequences. Measure maximum dimension in any plane for T staging.

Skip Metastases Recognition

Look for discontinuous tumor deposits within the same bone

These appear as separate enhancing masses within the primary bone, not continuous with main tumor.

Pelvic Segment Identification

Learn the four pelvic segments: ilium, ischium, pubis, sacrum

Count segments involved to determine T stage. Sacroiliac joint crossing = T4.

Extraosseous Extension

Assess soft tissue extension beyond bone cortex

Critical for pelvic staging. Use fat-suppressed sequences to detect subtle extension.

Spinal Canal Assessment

Carefully evaluate for spinal canal extension in spine tumors

Any extension into spinal canal = T4a. Use sagittal T2 and axial T1 post-contrast.

Vascular Involvement

Look for vessel encasement or tumor thrombus

Use contrast-enhanced sequences. >270° encasement suggests unresectability.

Chest CT for Metastases

Dedicated chest CT essential for staging workup

Lung is most common metastatic site. Use thin-section CT without contrast.

Bone Scan/PET Correlation

Whole-body imaging to detect bone metastases

PET-CT increasingly used. Correlate with anatomical imaging for accurate staging.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1
Confusing anatomical sites

Different T staging criteria apply to appendicular, pelvic, and spinal sites. Always identify the correct anatomical location first before applying staging criteria.

2
Missing skip metastases

Skip metastases appear as separate tumor deposits within the same bone. These automatically make the tumor T3 regardless of size, significantly affecting staging.

3
Incorrect pelvic segment counting

Learn the four pelvic segments (ilium, ischium, pubis, sacrum). Miscounting segments leads to incorrect T staging in pelvic tumors.

4
Overlooking extraosseous extension

Subtle soft tissue extension beyond bone cortex significantly affects pelvic T staging. Use fat-suppressed sequences to detect minimal extension.

5
Ignoring grade in final staging

Histologic grade is crucial for final stage grouping. Low-grade and high-grade tumors with identical TNM have different overall stages and prognoses.

6
Assuming lymph node involvement

Lymph node metastases are rare in bone sarcomas. Don't overcall reactive nodes as metastatic - requires histologic confirmation.

7
Inadequate metastatic workup

Always include chest CT and bone scan/PET in staging workup. Missing pulmonary metastases significantly understages the patient.

8-Step Systematic Reading Approach

1
Identify Anatomical Site

Determine if tumor is in appendicular skeleton, pelvis, or spine. This determines which T staging criteria to apply.

2
Measure Maximum Dimension

Measure tumor in all three planes on MRI. Record the maximum dimension - critical for T staging in all sites.

3
Assess for Skip Metastases

Look for discontinuous tumor deposits within the primary bone. If present, automatically T3 regardless of size.

4
Evaluate Local Extension

For pelvis: count segments and assess extraosseous extension. For spine: count vertebral segments and check spinal canal. For appendicular: assess cortical breakthrough.

5
Check for Vascular Involvement

Look for vessel encasement or tumor thrombus, especially in pelvic and spinal tumors. This indicates T4b disease.

6
Assess Regional Lymph Nodes

Examine regional lymph nodes, but remember metastases are rare in bone sarcomas. Don't overcall reactive nodes.

7
Evaluate for Distant Metastases

Review chest CT for pulmonary metastases and bone scan/PET for skeletal metastases. Lung is the most common site.

8
Correlate with Histology

Obtain histologic grade (low vs high) and tumor type. Grade significantly affects final stage grouping and prognosis.
