Key Imaging Tips

MRCP Before Stenting

Perform MRCP before biliary stent placement to avoid artifacts

Stents create susceptibility artifacts that can obscure tumor extent and biliary anatomy. Always advocate for pre-stent imaging when possible.

Bismuth-Corlette Classification

Use this system to describe biliary involvement

Type I: Below confluence, Type II: At confluence, Type IIIa: Right hepatic duct, Type IIIb: Left hepatic duct, Type IV: Bilateral involvement

Lobar Atrophy Assessment

Look for volume loss indicating chronic biliary obstruction

Atrophy suggests long-standing biliary obstruction and may indicate vascular involvement. Important for surgical planning and remnant liver volume calculation.

Vascular Encasement Criteria

Use >180° circumferential contact for arterial involvement

Portal vein: >50% circumference or occlusion. Hepatic artery: >180° contact or narrowing. Look for collateral vessels indicating chronic occlusion.

Periductal Enhancement Pattern

Cholangiocarcinoma shows delayed enhancement

Typically hypovascular on arterial phase with progressive enhancement on delayed phases. This helps differentiate from inflammatory strictures.

Regional Lymph Node Stations

Know the specific nodal stations for accurate N staging

Hilar, cystic duct, common bile duct, hepatic artery, posterior pancreatoduodenal, and portal vein nodes are considered regional. Size >1cm is suspicious.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1
Confusing T2a and T2b

T2a is adipose tissue invasion (beyond bile duct wall), T2b is hepatic parenchyma invasion. These are distinct anatomical compartments with different surgical implications.

2
Missing Bilateral Involvement

T4 requires bilateral portal vein branch involvement OR main portal vein. Don't miss contralateral involvement that upgrades staging significantly.

3
Overcalling Vascular Invasion

Simple contact is not invasion. Look for vessel narrowing, occlusion, or >50% circumferential involvement for portal vein, >180° for arteries.

4
Ignoring Lobar Atrophy

Atrophy indicates chronic biliary obstruction and often correlates with vascular involvement. Don't dismiss this important secondary sign.

5
Misidentifying Regional Nodes

Only specific nodal stations are regional. Para-aortic, celiac, and superior mesenteric nodes are distant metastases (M1), not regional (N1/N2).

6
Underestimating Periductal Spread

Cholangiocarcinoma spreads along bile ducts via perineural and lymphatic routes. Assess the full extent of biliary involvement, not just the dominant mass.

8-Step Systematic Reading Approach

1
Identify Primary Tumor Location

Confirm perihilar location at biliary confluence. Use Bismuth-Corlette classification to describe biliary involvement pattern.

2
Assess Local Extent

Determine if tumor is confined to bile duct (T1) or extends beyond wall to adipose tissue (T2a) or hepatic parenchyma (T2b).

3
Evaluate Vascular Involvement

Systematically assess portal vein and hepatic artery branches. Look for narrowing, occlusion, or circumferential involvement.

4
Check for Lobar Atrophy

Assess liver volume and look for atrophy patterns. This indicates chronic biliary obstruction and potential vascular compromise.

5
Map Biliary Extent

Use MRCP to determine proximal extent of biliary involvement. This affects surgical planning and resectability.

6
Survey Regional Lymph Nodes

Count and measure hilar, cystic duct, common bile duct, hepatic artery, and portal vein lymph nodes. Size >1cm is suspicious.

7
Screen for Distant Metastases

Check liver for metastases, assess peritoneum for deposits, and evaluate non-regional lymph nodes (para-aortic, celiac).

8
Synthesize Findings

Combine T, N, M assessments for overall stage. Consider resectability and provide clear recommendations for multidisciplinary team.
