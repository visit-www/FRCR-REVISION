Key Imaging Tips

Optimal CT Protocol

Use triphasic CT with pre-contrast, arterial, and delayed phases

Delayed phase (5-10 minutes) is crucial for detecting urothelial tumors and assessing invasion depth

MRI for Local Staging

T2-weighted images best show bladder wall layers

High-resolution T2WI can differentiate muscle invasion (T2) from perivesical fat invasion (T3)

Muscle Invasion Assessment

Look for loss of the hypointense muscle layer on T2WI

Intact dark muscle rim = T1 or less; disrupted muscle = T2 or higher

Perivesical Fat Invasion

Irregular, spiculated margins extending into fat

Smooth contours may be inflammatory; irregular margins suggest true invasion (T3)

Lymph Node Assessment

Size criteria: >8mm short axis for pelvic nodes

Also assess morphology - round nodes with loss of fatty hilum are suspicious

Distant Metastases

Common sites: liver, lung, bone, peritoneum

Include chest CT and consider bone scan for high-grade or muscle-invasive tumors

Post-Treatment Changes

BCG therapy can cause wall thickening and enhancement

Inflammatory changes can mimic invasion - correlate with treatment history

Ureterovesical Junction

Tumors near UVJ may involve ureter

Assess for ureteral involvement and hydronephrosis which may affect staging

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1
Overstaging due to inflammation

Post-biopsy or BCG-related inflammation can mimic muscle invasion. Always correlate with clinical history and consider timing of imaging relative to procedures.

2
Understaging small muscle-invasive tumors

Small T2 tumors may not show obvious muscle disruption. Look carefully for any loss of the dark muscle rim on T2WI, even if subtle.

3
Confusing perivesical fat stranding

Not all fat stranding indicates T3 disease. Smooth, regular margins suggest inflammation; irregular, spiculated margins suggest invasion.

4
Missing synchronous upper tract tumors

Urothelial cancer has a field defect. Always evaluate the entire urinary tract including renal pelvis and ureters on delayed imaging.

5
Inadequate lymph node assessment

Don't rely solely on size criteria. Assess node morphology, enhancement pattern, and consider PET/CT for equivocal cases.

6
Incorrect T4 staging criteria

T4 requires invasion of adjacent organs, not just contact. Look for loss of fat planes and direct tumor extension into the organ.

7
Ignoring variant histology implications

Micropapillary and plasmacytoid variants are more aggressive and may require different treatment approaches despite similar staging.

8-Step Systematic Reading Approach

1
Confirm tumor presence and location

Identify the primary tumor, note its location within the bladder, and assess for multifocality

2
Assess bladder wall layers

Evaluate urothelium, lamina propria, and muscularis propria on high-resolution T2WI or thin-section CT

3
Determine depth of invasion

Systematically assess for T1 (lamina propria), T2 (muscle), T3 (perivesical fat), or T4 (adjacent organs)

4
Evaluate perivesical fat

Look for irregular margins, spiculation, or nodular extension into perivesical fat indicating T3 disease

5
Assess adjacent organ involvement

Check prostate, seminal vesicles, uterus, vagina, pelvic sidewall, and abdominal wall for direct invasion

6
Evaluate regional lymph nodes

Assess pelvic lymph nodes (internal iliac, external iliac, obturator, presacral) for size and morphology

7
Screen for distant metastases

Evaluate liver, lungs, bones, and peritoneum for metastatic disease

8
Check entire urothelial tract

Assess renal pelvis, ureters, and urethra for synchronous tumors using delayed phase imaging
