Key Imaging Tips

MRCP vs ERCP

MRCP is non-invasive and excellent for initial assessment of bile duct anatomy and strictures.

ERCP allows for tissue sampling and therapeutic intervention but carries procedural risks.

Invasion Depth Measurement

Measure perpendicular to the bile duct wall on high-resolution T2-weighted images.

Use thin-section imaging (2-3mm) for accurate depth assessment.

Vascular Assessment

Evaluate celiac axis, SMA, and common hepatic artery on arterial phase imaging.

Look for >180° encasement or vessel narrowing/occlusion.

Regional Nodes

Include pancreaticoduodenal, pyloric, hepatic artery, and superior mesenteric nodes.

Size criteria less reliable than morphology - look for rounded, heterogeneous nodes.

Pancreatic Involvement

Distinguish distal bile duct tumors from pancreatic head tumors - epicenter is key.

Distal bile duct tumors center between cystic duct confluence and ampulla.

Liver Metastases

Best seen on portal venous phase as hypodense lesions against enhanced liver.

Small metastases may be subtle - use thin sections and compare phases.

Peritoneal Disease

Look for ascites, omental nodularity, and peritoneal thickening.

Small volume disease may only manifest as trace ascites.

Post-Stent Imaging

Stent artifacts can obscure tumor margins - pre-stent imaging preferred for staging.

If post-stent, use T2-weighted sequences to see through metallic artifacts.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1
Confusing with Pancreatic Cancer

Distal bile duct tumors in the pancreatic head can mimic pancreatic adenocarcinoma. The epicenter location is crucial - distal bile duct tumors center between the cystic duct confluence and ampulla, not in pancreatic parenchyma.

2
Incorrect Depth Measurement

Measuring invasion depth tangentially rather than perpendicular to the bile duct wall leads to overestimation. Always measure the shortest distance through the wall.

3
Missing Vascular Involvement

Subtle vascular encasement can be missed on single-phase imaging. Always evaluate major arteries on arterial phase with thin sections and multiplanar reconstructions.

4
Overcalling Lymph Nodes

Size alone is unreliable for nodal staging. Focus on morphology - rounded shape, loss of fatty hilum, and heterogeneous enhancement are more important than size.

5
Inadequate Liver Assessment

Small liver metastases are easily missed without proper technique. Use portal venous phase imaging with thin sections, and compare with arterial phase for small hypervascular lesions.

6
Ignoring Peritoneal Disease

Subtle peritoneal metastases may only manifest as small volume ascites or minimal omental thickening. These findings should prompt careful evaluation for M1 disease.

7
Post-Procedure Artifacts

Imaging after stent placement or biopsy can introduce artifacts and inflammatory changes that obscure tumor margins. Pre-intervention imaging is always preferred for staging.

8-Step Systematic Reading Approach

1
Confirm Tumor Location

Verify the epicenter is between cystic duct confluence and ampulla. Distinguish from pancreatic head or ampullary tumors.

2
Assess Bile Duct Wall

Measure invasion depth perpendicular to the wall. Use high-resolution T2-weighted images for best visualization of wall layers.

3
Evaluate Major Arteries

Systematically assess celiac axis, SMA, and common hepatic artery for encasement or narrowing on arterial phase.

4
Count Regional Nodes

Identify and count suspicious regional lymph nodes. Include pancreaticoduodenal, pyloric, hepatic artery, and superior mesenteric groups.

5
Screen for Liver Metastases

Carefully evaluate liver on portal venous phase. Look for hypodense lesions and compare with arterial phase for small lesions.

6
Check Peritoneal Surfaces

Look for ascites, omental nodularity, peritoneal thickening, or implants. Even trace ascites can be significant.

7
Assess Distant Sites

Evaluate lungs, bones, and other distant sites for metastatic disease. Include lung bases on abdominal imaging.

8
Synthesize Findings

Combine all findings to determine T, N, M stages and overall stage group. Consider resectability and treatment implications.
