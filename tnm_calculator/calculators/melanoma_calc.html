Key Imaging Tips

Primary Tumor Assessment

Focus on Breslow thickness measurement from histology, not imaging thickness.

Imaging cannot accurately measure Breslow thickness - rely on pathology report

Satellite vs In-Transit

Satellite: ≤2cm from primary; In-transit: >2cm from primary but before regional nodes.

Both are considered N1c if no nodal involvement, but staging differs with nodes

Lymph Node Assessment

Look for rounded morphology, loss of fatty hilum, and size >1cm in short axis.

Normal nodes can be up to 1cm; consider PET/CT for equivocal cases

Matted Lymph Nodes

Conglomerate mass of nodes with loss of individual node borders indicates N3.

Often associated with extranodal extension and poor prognosis

Brain Metastases

Always include brain MRI in staging workup for thick melanomas (>4mm).

Brain mets are M1d and carry worst prognosis - often excluded from trials

PET/CT Utility

Most useful for Stage III+ disease; limited value in thin melanomas.

False positives common in inflammatory conditions; correlate with CT findings

Liver Metastases

Melanoma mets are typically hypovascular and may be subtle on arterial phase.

Use portal venous phase and look for subtle hypoattenuating lesions

Lung Metastases

Can present as nodules, masses, or lymphangitic spread pattern.

Thin-section CT without contrast adequate for detection

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1
Confusing imaging thickness with Breslow thickness

Breslow thickness is a histologic measurement that cannot be accurately determined by imaging. Always refer to the pathology report for T staging.

2
Missing satellite/in-transit metastases

These small deposits can be subtle on imaging but significantly impact staging. Carefully examine the skin and subcutaneous tissues around the primary site.

3
Overlooking brain metastases

Brain MRI should be performed in high-risk cases. Brain metastases (M1d) have the worst prognosis and affect treatment eligibility.

4
Misclassifying reactive lymph nodes

Not all enlarged nodes are metastatic. Consider morphology, enhancement pattern, and clinical context. PET/CT can help in equivocal cases.

5
Ignoring LDH levels in Stage IV

Elevated LDH is an important prognostic factor in metastatic melanoma and affects treatment decisions. Always check and report LDH status.

6
Inadequate assessment of extranodal extension

Look for irregular node borders, stranding into adjacent fat, and loss of normal node architecture. ENE affects prognosis and treatment planning.

7
Confusing melanoma metastases with other lesions

Melanoma can metastasize anywhere and may be amelanotic. Consider melanoma in the differential for any unusual lesion in a patient with known melanoma.

8-Step Systematic Reading Approach

1
Review Clinical History

Check primary melanoma location, Breslow thickness, ulceration status, and prior treatments. Review pathology report for accurate T staging.

2
Assess Primary Site

Look for residual tumor, satellite lesions (≤2cm), and local recurrence. Examine surgical bed for complications or recurrence.

3
Evaluate In-Transit Disease

Systematically examine skin and subcutaneous tissues between primary and regional nodes for in-transit metastases (>2cm from primary).

4
Regional Lymph Node Assessment

Identify all regional node basins. Measure nodes, assess morphology, and look for matted nodes or extranodal extension.

5
Chest Evaluation

Examine lungs for nodules, masses, or lymphangitic spread. Assess mediastinal and hilar lymph nodes. Check for pleural effusion.

6
Abdominal Assessment

Focus on liver (portal venous phase), adrenals, and retroperitoneal nodes. Look for peritoneal deposits and bowel involvement.

7
Brain and Spine Evaluation

If brain MRI performed, look for parenchymal lesions, leptomeningeal disease, and hemorrhage. Check spine for bone metastases.

8
Correlate with Laboratory

Check LDH levels for prognostic information in Stage IV disease. Review other tumor markers if available.
