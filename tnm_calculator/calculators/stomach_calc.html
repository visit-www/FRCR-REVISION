Key Imaging Tips

Gastric Wall Layer Assessment

Use thin-section CT with oral and IV contrast to evaluate wall layers

Look for loss of normal wall stratification. Normal gastric wall should show distinct mucosal, submucosal, and muscular layers. Tumor invasion disrupts this pattern.

Serosal Invasion (T4a) Signs

Identify serosal enhancement, nodularity, or fat stranding

Serosal invasion appears as enhancement or nodularity of the visceral peritoneum. Look for irregular outer gastric contour and adjacent fat stranding.

Adjacent Organ Invasion (T4b)

Look for loss of fat planes and direct organ involvement

T4b requires direct invasion, not just abutment. Look for loss of fat planes, organ deformity, and continuous tumor extension into adjacent structures.

Regional Lymph Node Stations

Know the gastric lymph node drainage patterns

Regional nodes include perigastric, left gastric, common hepatic, splenic, and celiac nodes. Nodes >1cm short axis or with abnormal enhancement are suspicious.

Peritoneal Metastases

Carefully evaluate for peritoneal carcinomatosis

Look for peritoneal nodules, ascites, omental caking, and bowel wall thickening. Small volume disease may be subtle on CT but visible on MRI.

Liver Metastases Detection

Use portal venous phase for optimal liver lesion detection

Gastric cancer liver metastases are typically hypovascular. Portal venous phase provides best contrast between metastases and normal liver parenchyma.

EGJ Tumor Location

Precisely locate tumors relative to the esophagogastric junction

Tumors ≤2cm from EGJ use esophageal staging. Tumors >2cm from EGJ use gastric staging. Accurate measurement is crucial for proper staging schema selection.

Optimal CT Technique

Use gastric distension and appropriate contrast timing

Oral contrast (water or positive contrast) distends the stomach. IV contrast with portal venous phase timing optimizes tumor and nodal visualization.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1
Confusing Abutment with Invasion

T4b requires actual invasion, not just contact. Look for loss of fat planes, organ deformity, and continuous tumor extension. Simple abutment without invasion is not T4b.

2
Missing Peritoneal Disease

Small volume peritoneal metastases can be subtle. Carefully examine the pelvis, paracolic gutters, and omentum. Consider MRI if CT is equivocal.

3
Inadequate Gastric Distension

Poor gastric distension can simulate wall thickening or miss small lesions. Ensure adequate oral contrast and proper timing for optimal gastric distension.

4
Wrong Staging Schema for EGJ Tumors

Measure distance from EGJ precisely. Tumors ≤2cm from EGJ use esophageal staging, >2cm use gastric staging. This significantly affects treatment planning.

5
Overcalling Lymph Nodes

Not all enlarged nodes are metastatic. Consider reactive changes, especially in the setting of gastritis or ulceration. Correlation with morphology and enhancement is important.

6
Missing Liver Metastases

Gastric cancer liver metastases can be small and hypovascular. Use portal venous phase imaging and consider MRI for better detection of small lesions.

7
Confusing Clinical vs Pathological Staging

Clinical staging (cTNM) is based on imaging and differs from pathological staging (pTNM). Use appropriate stage groupings for clinical staging.

8
Ignoring Distant Nodal Stations

Supraclavicular, mediastinal, or retroperitoneal nodes outside the regional drainage are M1 disease. Don't forget to evaluate these areas systematically.

8-Step Systematic Reading Approach

1
Locate the Primary Tumor

Identify tumor location within the stomach. Measure distance from EGJ if in the cardia/fundus region to determine appropriate staging schema.

2
Assess Gastric Wall Layers

Evaluate depth of invasion through gastric wall layers: mucosa/submucosa (T1), muscularis propria (T2), subserosa (T3), serosa (T4a).

3
Check for Adjacent Organ Invasion

Systematically evaluate all adjacent structures: pancreas, spleen, colon, liver, diaphragm, kidney, adrenal, abdominal wall, retroperitoneum for T4b.

4
Count Regional Lymph Nodes

Identify and count suspicious regional lymph nodes in perigastric, left gastric, common hepatic, splenic, and celiac regions.

5
Search for Peritoneal Disease

Carefully examine peritoneal surfaces, omentum, pelvis, and paracolic gutters for peritoneal carcinomatosis (M1 disease).

6
Evaluate Liver for Metastases

Assess liver parenchyma for metastatic lesions using portal venous phase imaging. Consider MRI if CT findings are equivocal.

7
Check Distant Sites

Evaluate lungs, bones, distant lymph nodes (supraclavicular, mediastinal, retroperitoneal) for distant metastases.

8
Synthesize and Stage

Combine T, N, and M assessments to determine overall clinical stage. Consider treatment implications and discuss multidisciplinary approach.
