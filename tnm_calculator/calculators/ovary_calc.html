Key Imaging Tips for Staging

Capsule Integrity Assessment

Look for smooth, intact ovarian/tubal contours on T2-weighted images.

Irregular contours, surface nodularity, or fluid signal around the mass suggest capsule breach (T1c2).

Peritoneal Disease Detection

Use fat-suppressed T1 post-contrast sequences to identify small peritoneal implants.

Look in dependent portions: pouch of Douglas, paracolic gutters, and right subphrenic space.

Liver Involvement Distinction

Differentiate capsular (T3c) from parenchymal (M1b) liver involvement.

Capsular disease appears as surface nodules; parenchymal disease shows discrete intraparenchymal lesions.

Lymph Node Assessment

Evaluate pelvic and para-aortic nodes systematically on axial images.

Short axis >1cm is suspicious; look for central necrosis and irregular enhancement patterns.

Ascites Evaluation

Assess ascites volume and distribution pattern for staging implications.

Large volume ascites often indicates advanced disease; obtain cytology when possible.

Pleural Assessment

Include lung bases in abdominal imaging to detect pleural effusions.

Pleural effusion with positive cytology = M1a disease (Stage IVA).

Bowel Involvement

Look for bowel wall thickening and serosal implants on mesenteric surfaces.

Distinguish serosal implants (T3) from full-thickness bowel wall invasion requiring surgical assessment.

Diaphragmatic Disease

Assess diaphragmatic surfaces for nodular thickening or masses.

Right hemidiaphragm more commonly involved; may require surgical staging confirmation.

Common Staging Pitfalls to Avoid

1
Confusing Capsular vs. Parenchymal Liver Disease

Liver capsule involvement is T3c (Stage IIIC), while parenchymal metastases are M1b (Stage IVB). Always distinguish surface nodules from intraparenchymal lesions.

2
Missing Small Peritoneal Implants

Small peritoneal deposits can be subtle on imaging. Use fat-suppressed post-contrast sequences and look systematically in all peritoneal recesses.

3
Overlooking T1c Subcategories

T1c has three important subcategories (surgical spill, pre-op rupture, positive cytology) that affect prognosis. Document capsule integrity carefully.

4
Inadequate Lymph Node Assessment

Both pelvic and para-aortic nodes are regional. Positive nodes in T1-T2 disease = Stage IIIA1, not Stage I or II.

5
Misclassifying Pleural Effusions

Only pleural effusions with positive cytology are M1a. Simple effusions without malignant cells don't upstage the disease.

6
Ignoring Bilateral Disease

Bilateral ovarian involvement without other features is still T1b if capsules are intact. Don't automatically assume higher T stage.

7
Confusing Pelvic vs. Extrapelvic Disease

Pelvic extension (uterus, tubes, pelvic peritoneum) is T2, while extrapelvic peritoneum is T3. Know your pelvic anatomy boundaries.

8-Step Systematic Staging Approach

1
Identify Primary Site

Determine if the primary is ovarian, fallopian tube, or peritoneal. Look for the dominant mass and pattern of spread.

2
Assess Capsule Integrity

Evaluate ovarian/tubal surface contours on T2-weighted images. Look for surface irregularity or nodularity suggesting capsule breach.

3
Evaluate Pelvic Extension

Check for extension to uterus, contralateral adnexa, and pelvic peritoneum. This determines T2 disease.

4
Search for Extrapelvic Peritoneal Disease

Systematically evaluate all peritoneal surfaces: paracolic gutters, subphrenic spaces, lesser sac, mesentery, and omentum.

5
Assess Liver and Spleen

Distinguish capsular involvement (T3c) from parenchymal metastases (M1b). Look at surface contours and intraparenchymal lesions separately.

6
Evaluate Regional Lymph Nodes

Assess pelvic (internal iliac, external iliac, obturator) and para-aortic lymph nodes. Size >1cm short axis is suspicious.

7
Check for Distant Metastases

Evaluate pleural spaces, lung parenchyma, liver parenchyma, and other distant organs. Include lung bases in abdominal scans.

8
Correlate with Clinical Information

Consider surgical findings, cytology results, and CA-125 levels. Imaging findings should be correlated with clinical context.
