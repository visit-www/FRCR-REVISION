Key Imaging Tips

Extraprostatic Extension (EPE)

Look for capsular irregularity, bulging, or frank extension

MRI signs: Asymmetric bulging of prostatic contour, irregular capsular margin, direct tumor extension into periprostatic fat, neurovascular bundle thickening >2mm

Seminal Vesicle Invasion

Direct tumor extension into seminal vesicles

MRI signs: Low T2 signal in SV, loss of normal SV architecture, direct tumor extension from prostate base, obliteration of angle between prostate and SV

Bladder Neck Involvement

Distinguish bladder neck from bladder wall invasion

Key point: Microscopic bladder neck invasion = T3a (pathological only). Gross bladder wall invasion = T4. Look for tumor at the bladder neck on sagittal images

Regional vs Nonregional Nodes

Know the regional node boundaries

Regional: Obturator, internal/external iliac, presacral, sacral, common iliac. Nonregional: Inguinal, superficial inguinal, supraclavicular, mediastinal, para-aortic above bifurcation

Bone Metastases Detection

Prostate cancer has predilection for bone

Imaging: Bone scan most sensitive for multiple lesions. MRI/CT for characterization. Look for sclerotic lesions in axial skeleton (spine, pelvis, ribs). PSMA PET increasingly used

Rectal Wall Assessment

Evaluate for T4 rectal invasion

MRI technique: Use high-resolution T2 axial images. Look for loss of fat plane between prostate and rectum, direct tumor extension, rectal wall thickening/irregularity

Pelvic Sidewall Invasion

T4 feature - tumor extends to pelvic sidewall

Assessment: Look for tumor extension to obturator internus, piriformis, or levator ani muscles. Loss of fat planes. Direct muscle invasion or encasement

PSMA PET Integration

Increasingly used for staging and restaging

Applications: Superior to conventional imaging for nodal staging, bone metastases detection, and biochemical recurrence. Particularly useful for oligometastatic disease assessment

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1
Confusing Clinical vs Pathological T2 Staging

Clinical T2 has subdivisions (T2a/b/c) based on extent, but pathological T2 does not. All organ-confined disease at surgery is pT2, regardless of extent or laterality.

2
Mistaking Bladder Neck for Bladder Wall

Microscopic bladder neck invasion is T3a (pathological only), while gross bladder wall invasion is T4. The bladder neck is the junction between bladder and prostate.

3
Overcalling EPE on MRI

Benign prostatic hyperplasia can cause capsular bulging. True EPE requires irregular capsular contour with tumor signal extending beyond the capsule. Use DWI and dynamic contrast for confirmation.

4
Missing Anterior Tumors

Anterior/transition zone tumors may be missed on DRE and TRUS. MRI is superior for detecting anterior tumors. These can still be T2 if organ-confined.

5
Incorrect Regional Node Classification

Para-aortic nodes above the bifurcation are M1a (nonregional), not N1. Know the boundaries: regional nodes are limited to pelvis below common iliac bifurcation.

6
Ignoring Grade Group in Stage Grouping

AJCC 8th/9th edition incorporates Grade Group and PSA into stage grouping. High-grade (GG 5) or high PSA (>20) can upstage to Group III even with organ-confined disease.

7
Misinterpreting Post-Biopsy Changes

Recent biopsy can cause hemorrhage and inflammation, mimicking EPE on MRI. Wait 6-8 weeks post-biopsy for optimal MRI staging, or use DWI to differentiate tumor from hemorrhage.

8
Understaging Seminal Vesicle Invasion

SV invasion can be subtle on imaging. Look carefully at the angle between prostate base and SV. Any tumor extension into SV = T3b, regardless of the extent of invasion.

8-Step Systematic Reading Approach

1
Clinical Context Review

Review PSA level, DRE findings, biopsy results (Gleason score/Grade Group), and indication for imaging. Determine if clinical or pathological staging is needed.

2
Primary Tumor Identification

Locate the primary tumor on T2WI. Assess size, location (peripheral vs transition zone), and signal characteristics. Use DWI and DCE to confirm tumor location.

3
Capsular Assessment

Systematically evaluate the prostatic capsule for irregularity, bulging, or frank extension. Look for loss of the normal hypointense capsular rim on T2WI.

4
Seminal Vesicle Evaluation

Assess SV architecture and signal intensity. Look for loss of normal T2 hyperintense signal, asymmetry, or direct tumor extension from prostate base.

5
Adjacent Structure Assessment

Evaluate bladder wall, rectal wall, pelvic sidewall muscles (levator ani, obturator internus), and external sphincter for invasion. Look for loss of fat planes.

6
Lymph Node Evaluation

Assess regional nodes (obturator, internal/external iliac, presacral, sacral, common iliac) for size, morphology, and signal characteristics. Size >8mm is suspicious.

7
Distant Metastases Search

Review bone marrow signal on MRI for metastases. Assess for nonregional nodes, liver lesions, and other distant sites. Correlate with bone scan if available.

8
Final TNM Assignment

Integrate all findings to assign T, N, and M stages. Consider clinical parameters (PSA, Grade Group) for overall stage grouping. Document confidence level and recommendations.
