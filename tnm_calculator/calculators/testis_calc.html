Key Imaging Tips

Retroperitoneal Node Assessment

Primary drainage is to para-aortic and paracaval nodes. Right testis drains to paracaval and interaortocaval nodes; left testis drains to para-aortic nodes.

Use short-axis measurement for nodal size. Normal retroperitoneal nodes are typically <10mm.

Pulmonary Metastases

Most common site of distant metastases. Can be multiple small nodules or larger masses. Always obtain chest CT for staging.

Small pulmonary nodules may be the only sign of metastatic disease in early stages.

Liver Metastases

Indicates M1b disease. Usually multiple and may be hypervascular on arterial phase imaging, especially in choriocarcinoma.

Consider multiphase CT or MRI for better characterization of liver lesions.

Brain Metastases

More common with choriocarcinoma and embryonal carcinoma. Indicates M1b disease and poor prognosis.

Consider brain MRI in patients with high β-hCG levels or neurological symptoms.

Bone Metastases

Uncommon but indicates M1b disease. Usually osteolytic lesions. Consider bone scan or whole-body MRI if suspected.

Spine is the most common site of bone metastases in testicular cancer.

Mediastinal Nodes

Considered distant metastases (M1a). Common in advanced disease. May cause superior vena cava syndrome.

Measure in short axis; >10mm is considered abnormal in the mediastinum.

Post-Chemotherapy Changes

Residual masses >10mm after chemotherapy may contain viable tumor, mature teratoma, or necrosis. Size reduction is a good prognostic sign.

PET-CT has limited utility in post-chemotherapy assessment due to mature teratoma.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1
Confusing Hilar Invasion with Spermatic Cord Invasion

Hilar soft tissue invasion is pT2, while spermatic cord invasion is pT3. The hilum is within the testis where tubules converge; the spermatic cord is external to the testis.

2
Missing Lymphovascular Invasion

LVI in a testis-limited tumor makes it pT2, not pT1. This is crucial for treatment decisions, especially in seminoma patients considering adjuvant therapy.

3
Incorrect Node Measurement

Always measure the greatest dimension of the nodal mass, not individual nodes. A conglomerate mass should be measured as a single entity.

4
Overlooking Extranodal Extension

In pathological staging, extranodal extension automatically makes it pN2, regardless of size or number of nodes. Look for tumor extending beyond the nodal capsule.

5
Misclassifying Mediastinal Nodes

Mediastinal lymph nodes are considered distant metastases (M1a), not regional nodes. Don't confuse them with retroperitoneal nodes.

6
Ignoring Clinical vs Pathological Differences

Clinical staging has limited T categories (cTX, cT0, cTis, cT4 only). Most T staging requires pathological assessment after orchiectomy.

7
Forgetting Serum Tumor Markers

While not part of TNM staging, serum markers (AFP, β-hCG, LDH) are crucial for prognosis and treatment decisions. Always correlate with clinical findings.

8-Step Systematic Reading Approach

1
Confirm Primary Site

Identify the testicular mass and assess for bilateral disease. Note any scrotal wall involvement or skin changes.

2
Assess Retroperitoneal Nodes

Systematically evaluate para-aortic, paracaval, and interaortocaval nodes. Measure the largest nodal mass in greatest dimension.

3
Evaluate Chest

Look for pulmonary nodules or masses. Assess mediastinal and hilar lymph nodes. Consider pleural involvement.

4
Check for Liver Metastases

Examine liver on all phases if contrast-enhanced. Look for hypervascular lesions, especially in choriocarcinoma.

5
Assess Other Distant Sites

Evaluate bones, brain (if indicated), and other organs for metastases. Consider supraclavicular and inguinal nodes.

6
Correlate with Serum Markers

Review AFP, β-hCG, and LDH levels. High markers may indicate occult metastases or specific histological subtypes.

7
Review Pathology (if available)

Correlate imaging with histopathological findings. Note tumor size, LVI, and local invasion patterns.

8
Assign Final Stage

Combine T, N, and M stages to determine overall stage. Consider prognostic factors and treatment implications.
