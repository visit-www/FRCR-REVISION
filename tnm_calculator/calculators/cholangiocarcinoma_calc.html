Key Imaging Tips

Arterial Phase Enhancement

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma typically shows rim enhancement in arterial phase with progressive centripetal fill-in.

Look for the characteristic "target sign" - central hypointensity with peripheral enhancement

Capsular Retraction

Capsular retraction is a characteristic feature of cholangiocarcinoma due to its desmoplastic nature.

This helps differentiate from hepatocellular carcinoma which typically causes capsular bulging

Biliary Dilatation

Look for upstream biliary dilatation, which may be subtle in peripheral lesions.

MRCP can be particularly helpful in demonstrating biliary involvement

Vascular Assessment

Carefully evaluate portal and hepatic veins for encasement or invasion on portal venous phase.

Use multiplanar reconstructions to assess vessel involvement accurately

Lymph Node Evaluation

Regional lymph nodes include hilar, hepatic artery, portal vein, and aortocaval nodes.

Size criteria: >1cm short axis for most locations, >0.8cm for hepatic artery nodes

Peritoneal Assessment

Look for peritoneal enhancement, nodules, or ascites suggesting peritoneal involvement.

Peritoneal perforation upgrades to T4 and significantly impacts prognosis

Satellite Lesions

Distinguish between satellite lesions (same segment) and separate tumor nodules (different segments).

Multiple tumors in different segments are staged as T2 or T3 depending on size

Adjacent Organ Invasion

Assess for direct invasion of diaphragm, adrenal gland, kidney, or colon.

Loss of fat planes alone is insufficient - look for actual tissue invasion

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1
Confusing with HCC Staging

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma has different staging criteria than hepatocellular carcinoma. Don't use HCC staging rules for cholangiocarcinoma.

2
Missing Vascular Invasion

Carefully assess for portal and hepatic vein invasion, which upgrades solitary tumors >2cm from T1b to T2.

3
Incorrect Multiple Tumor Staging

For multiple tumors, staging depends on the size of the largest lesion (T2 if all ≤5cm, T3 if any >5cm), not the number of lesions.

4
Overlooking Peritoneal Involvement

Peritoneal perforation or implants automatically make the tumor T4, regardless of size or other features.

5
Inadequate Lymph Node Assessment

Don't forget to evaluate all regional lymph node stations, including aortocaval and retrocaval nodes.

6
Misinterpreting Enhancement Patterns

The rim enhancement pattern can be subtle - use delayed phases to confirm progressive centripetal enhancement.

7
Gallbladder Exception

Direct invasion of the gallbladder does NOT qualify as T4 (adjacent organ invasion) - only other adjacent organs count.

8-Step Systematic Reading Approach

1
Confirm Diagnosis

Verify this is intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, not HCC or metastasis. Look for characteristic rim enhancement and capsular retraction.

2
Count and Measure Tumors

Determine if solitary or multiple tumors. Measure the largest dimension of each lesion accurately.

3
Assess Vascular Involvement

Evaluate portal and hepatic veins for encasement, narrowing, or invasion. Use multiplanar reconstructions.

4
Check Adjacent Organ Invasion

Look for direct invasion of diaphragm, adrenal gland, kidney, colon, or other adjacent structures (excluding gallbladder).

5
Evaluate Peritoneal Surfaces

Check for peritoneal enhancement, nodules, or perforation. Look for ascites and peritoneal thickening.

6
Assess Regional Lymph Nodes

Systematically evaluate hilar, hepatic artery, portal vein, IVC, and aortocaval lymph nodes for enlargement or abnormal enhancement.

7
Search for Distant Metastases

Check lungs, bones, peritoneum, and other organs for metastatic disease. Include non-regional lymph nodes.

8
Determine Final Stage

Combine T, N, and M stages according to AJCC criteria to determine overall stage and treatment implications.
