Phases of contrast enhancement

After IV iodinated contrast, six distinct phases can be imaged. Timing is measured from the start of injection (p.i.) or from bolus tracking trigger.

Phase Timing (p.i.) Shows Clinical use
Non-enhanced (NECT) Baseline — no contrast Calcifications, fat in tumours, fat-stranding, acute haemorrhage Appendicitis, diverticulitis, omental infarction, stone disease, acute stroke
Early arterial 15–20 s p.i. or immediately after bolus tracking Contrast in arteries only — parenchyma not yet enhanced Arterial visualisation for CT angiography
Late arterial 35–40 s p.i. or 15–20 s after bolus tracking Optimal arterial supply to organs; hypervascular lesion conspicuity HCC, FNH, adenoma, haemangioma, hypervascular metastases (RCC, NET, thyroid, melanoma, choriocarcinoma)
Hepatic / late portal venous 70–80 s p.i. or 50–60 s after bolus tracking Liver parenchyma fully enhanced via portal vein; hepatic veins visible Hypovascular lesion detection (metastases, abscess, cyst), general surveillance CAP
Nephrogenic 100 s p.i. or 80 s after bolus tracking Complete renal parenchymal enhancement including medulla Small RCC detection; confirming enhancement of indeterminate renal lesion
Delayed / equilibrium 6–10 min p.i. Contrast washout from normal tissue; retention in fibrotic tissue Cholangiocarcinoma, fibrotic metastases, adrenal washout, scar/fibrosis
Bolus tracking vs fixed delay: bolus tracking (ROI placed in aorta, trigger at ~100 HU) is more reproducible across patients than a fixed delay, because it automatically compensates for differences in cardiac output, injection site, and cannula calibre.
Timing of CECT — how scanner speed, rate, volume & delay interact

Pick your scanner and patient weight — the calculator shows the recommended volume, injection rate, and scan start time for the late arterial and portal venous phases.

Key interactions:
  • Faster scanner → wait longer. A 64-slice scanner finishes the liver in ~4 s, so start closer to the target phase centre. A single-slice scanner takes ~20 s, so start earlier so the acquisition straddles the target time.
  • Higher rate → earlier peak. At 5 mL/s the aortic peak arrives ~5 s earlier than at 3 mL/s for the same volume.
  • Higher volume → longer plateau. 150 mL gives a longer steady-state window than 100 mL, useful for dual-energy or split-bolus techniques.
  • Cardiac output matters. Poor CO delays the bolus by 5–10 s. Use bolus tracking rather than fixed delay in cardiac failure.
Oral contrast — types, indications & caveats

Three broad classes based on attenuation. Choice depends on whether you need to mark the bowel lumen or preserve the ability to see bowel-wall enhancement.

Type Examples Attenuation When to use Caveats
Positive Dilute iodinated contrast (Gastrografin / Urografin), dilute barium sulphate High (+100 to +400 HU) Suspected bowel perforation, anastomotic leak, fistula tracking, differentiation of collapsed bowel from a mass Partial bowel filling; obscures bowel-wall enhancement; higher radiation dose required to see through high HU; avoid before CT colonography
Neutral Water, methylcellulose, polyethylene glycol (PEG), dilute (2 %) mannitol, Volumen® (low-density barium) Low (~0 HU) CT enterography / enteroclysis, mesenteric ischaemia, acute abdomen when bowel-wall enhancement needed Needs cooperative patient (large volume, timed drinking); mannitol can cause diarrhoea and cramping
Negative Air, CO₂ insufflation Very low (−1000 HU) CT colonography, virtual colonoscopy, rarely stomach distension Requires rectal tube and insufflator; patient discomfort; not for perforation workup
Acute abdomen / appendicitis: most modern protocols use no oral contrast or water only — delay from oral contrast (45–90 min) is unacceptable, and IV contrast + thin-section MDCT is diagnostic without it.
Water loading trick: 500–1000 mL water 30–60 min before scan distends the stomach and proximal small bowel, improving visualisation of the gastric and pancreatic regions without obscuring bowel-wall enhancement.
Rectal contrast — when to use it

Rectal contrast is retrograde filling of the colon/rectum via a rectal tube. Positive contrast (water-soluble iodinated) is standard. Reserve for specific indications.

Preparation
  • Dilution: 50 mL non-ionic water-soluble iodinated contrast in 750 mL water (≈ 6 % dilution).
  • Volume: up to 800 mL instilled via rectal tube — stop when flow stops, at 350 mL, or when patient can no longer tolerate.
  • Timing: instil immediately before scanning; IV contrast given right after rectal instillation.
  • Post-scan: enter the room promptly and release rectal contrast.
Indications
  • Suspected anastomotic leak (post-colorectal surgery) — most common indication.
  • Penetrating abdominal trauma — to detect occult bowel perforation when there is no other indication for laparotomy.
  • Colonic fistula (enterocutaneous, colovesical, colovaginal).
  • Bladder rupture — retrograde CT cystogram technique (via urinary catheter + 40 cm head of water, not rectal).
  • CT colonography uses gaseous (negative) rectal distension, not positive rectal contrast.
Contraindications: recent distal anastomosis (< 7 days) unless specifically looking for leak; severe proctitis; suspected rectal perforation without surgical backup.
Liver dual blood supply & lesion characterisation

Normal liver parenchyma is supplied 80 % by the portal vein and only 20 % by the hepatic artery. Virtually all liver tumours get 100 % of their supply from the hepatic artery. This physiological asymmetry is the basis for multiphasic liver imaging.

Why arterial phase matters: at 35 s, normal parenchyma has received almost none of its supply (portal vein is just starting), so any hyperenhancing lesion stands out maximally. Miss the arterial window and a hypervascular lesion becomes invisible (iso-attenuating) on the portal venous phase.
